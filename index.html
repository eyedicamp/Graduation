Adaptive Gating of EHR and PRS for 5-Year CVD Risk Prediction

An interpretable multimodal framework for integrating electronic health records and polygenic risk scores in low-prevalence cardiovascular risk prediction.

Overview

Abstract

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk prediction may benefit from combining electronic health records (EHR) with genetic information, but many models assume uniform polygenic risk score (PRS) contribution and focus mainly on discrimination. This study develops an interpretable framework for 5-year CVD risk prediction in UK Biobank by adaptively integrating structured EHR features and an 11-component multi-PRS representation. The cohort includes 388,746 White-ancestry participants, with 8,900 incident events defined as first myocardial infarction, stroke, or heart failure within five years after baseline. A two-expert gating model produces separate EHR- and PRS-based predictions and learns an individual-specific PRS reliance weight. The framework is compared with logistic regression, tree-based, and neural fusion baselines using multiple seeds and a fixed held-out test set. Isotonic calibration is applied seed-wise and evaluated through test-time ensembling. AUROC values are similar across models at around 0.78, and concatenated logistic regression achieves the highest mean AUROC. Although the gating model does not show a clear discrimination advantage, it provides patient-specific PRS reliance estimates and modest differences in risk stratification and decision-curve net benefit. Overall, adaptive gating offers an interpretable and calibrated approach for EHR–PRS integration and supports evaluation beyond discrimination alone.

Why this study?

Research Motivation

Clinical background

Cardiovascular disease remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Risk prediction is important because it can support early identification of patients who may need more intensive prevention, follow-up, or treatment.

In this thesis, the 5-year horizon is treated as clinically actionable: long enough to capture meaningful incident events, but short enough to inform near- to mid-term decision making.

Core research question

Can EHR and PRS be integrated in a patient-specific and interpretable way, rather than assuming that genetic information contributes equally to everyone?

Main claim: multimodal CVD prediction should be selective, calibrated, and interpretable.

Two-expert gating model

  • PRS expert: produces a genetic risk logit using PRS only
  • EHR expert: produces a clinical risk logit using EHR only
  • Gating network: predicts an individual-specific PRS reliance weight, wPRS
  • Final prediction: combines the two logits using the learned reliance weight

Interpretation of the gate

wPRS represents individual-level genetic reliance.

  • Closer to 0 → more reliance on the EHR expert
  • Closer to 1 → more reliance on the PRS expert

This makes the fusion mechanism directly interpretable instead of hiding modality contribution inside a single opaque model.

Calibration takeaway

After class-weighted training, raw predicted probabilities were much larger than the actual event prevalence. Seed-wise isotonic calibration substantially improved Brier score and qECE while keeping AUROC and AUPRC nearly unchanged.

However, calibration also contracted the prediction distribution toward the low-prevalence event rate, making probabilities more reliable but less sharp.

What the model reveals

Interpretation and Clinical Meaning

PRS reliance pattern

The learned wPRS captures how much PRS contributes to each patient’s final prediction.

  • Higher in men than in women
  • Tends to increase with age
  • Shows that PRS was not used uniformly across individuals

Feature attribution

  • SHAP: age, sex, cystatin C, and CAD-related PRS were important signals
  • Integrated gradients: CAD, ischemic stroke, and heart-failure-related PRS components were influential inside the PRS expert
  • The model used both clinically meaningful EHR signals and disease-relevant genetic signals
Final interpretation: PRS should not be treated as information that is added identically to every patient. Instead, the findings support the idea that genetic risk should be used selectively, depending on the individual.

Caveats

Limitations and Future Work

Limitations

  • Population generalizability is limited because the analysis focused on a White-ancestry subset of UK Biobank.
  • Evaluation was based on internal train/validation/test splits rather than an external independent cohort.

Future work

  • External validation in independent cohorts
  • Validation in diverse ancestry groups
  • Assessment of transportability under dataset shift
  • Extension to richer longitudinal EHR and broader genetic representations
